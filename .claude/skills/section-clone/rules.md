# HUMAN-DESIGNED PRODUCT UI — binding rules

Read fully before designing. Every rule here is a hard constraint from the product owner.
Purpose: interfaces that feel intentionally created by an experienced product designer.

The output must NOT look like: AI-generated SaaS UI, generic Tailwind templates, Dribbble
concept shots with no real UX logic, copied Figma community templates, generic startup landing
pages, "modern AI startup" visual clichés.

The design should feel: deliberate, restrained, functional, brand-specific, product-aware,
visually mature, coherent across screens, human-made. Every design choice must have a reason.

## Where the "AI look" comes from (all are red flags)

Predictable copy · predictable section order · generic component choices · excessive rounded
cards · fake product claims · repeated icon-card patterns · gradients/glows as decoration ·
weak typography hierarchy · too much symmetry · overuse of pills/badges · generic SaaS
language · decorative UI with no product logic · repetitive punctuation such as em dashes ·
layouts that look like Tailwind templates.

---

## 1. Never default to generic AI UI

Do not automatically generate: giant gradient hero sections; purple/blue glowing backgrounds;
floating blurry blobs; glassmorphism everywhere; excessive backdrop blur; neon borders; cards
floating in empty space; endless rounded rectangles; 3-column icon-card sections; random bento
grids; oversized pill-shaped buttons; icon-inside-colored-rounded-square for every feature;
fake dashboard mockups; random browser frames; meaningless analytics charts; fake avatars;
fake company logos; fake social proof; fake numbers; fake testimonials; fake user counts; fake
revenue numbers; fake integrations; generic star-rating blocks; decorative UI that does not
serve the product.

Do not use a trendy visual pattern merely because it is popular. Use it only if it solves a
real design problem.

## 2. Avoid the classic AI landing-page structure

Do not blindly generate: Navbar → Hero → logo cloud → 3 feature cards → bento grid → how it
works → testimonials → pricing → FAQ → CTA → footer. That sequence is extremely predictable.

Understand the product first. Build page structure around user intent, product value, actual
workflow, decision-making sequence, and information hierarchy. Different products should
produce different page structures.

## 3. Copy must not sound AI-generated

Banned phrases (and their close cousins): "Unlock the power of…", "Transform your workflow",
"Supercharge your productivity", "Elevate your experience", "Seamlessly integrate",
"Effortlessly manage", "Revolutionize the way you…", "Empower your team", "Everything you
need", "Built for modern teams", "Work smarter, not harder", "Take your productivity to the
next level", "Your all-in-one solution", "One platform. Endless possibilities.", "The future
of…", "Powerful. Simple. Fast.", "Designed for everyone", "AI-powered innovation", "Say
goodbye to…", "Meet your new…", "Experience the difference", "Built to scale with you", "From
chaos to clarity", "Turn X into Y", "Your work, reimagined".

Do not create exaggerated claims without evidence. Do not invent statistics, percentages,
customer counts, company logos, reviews, awards, performance claims, security certifications,
or testimonials.

Use specific product language.
Bad: "Transform every conversation into actionable intelligence."
Better: "Record the meeting, find the decision, and jump back to the exact moment it was
made." Prefer concrete verbs over marketing abstractions.

## 4. Avoid AI writing punctuation habits

Do not overuse em dashes, semicolons, colon-heavy headings, ellipses, slash-heavy phrases,
excessive parentheses, or sentence fragments for artificial drama.

Especially avoid patterns like "Fast — simple — powerful." / "Your meetings — organized,
searchable, actionable." / "One workspace — every conversation."

Use normal human sentence structure. Em dashes are allowed occasionally when genuinely
appropriate, never as a repeated stylistic signature. Do not make every heading
"Something. Something else." Do not artificially break simple sentences into dramatic
fragments.

## 5. Typography must feel designed

Do not rely only on font-size + font-weight. Create hierarchy with type scale, line height,
letter spacing, width, alignment, whitespace, contrast, and density.

Avoid: every title huge; every heading bold; excessive 700/800 weights; 72px+ headings without
reason; uppercase micro-labels everywhere; excessive letter spacing; gradient text by default.

A professional interface usually needs fewer font sizes than an AI-generated one. One primary
typeface unless a second has a clear editorial reason.

## 6. Don't overuse cards

Do not put heading, feature, statistic, testimonial, integration, action, user, navigation,
and CTA each in a card on the same page. Ask: "Does this information actually need a
container?" Prefer whitespace, alignment, dividers, typography, grouping, columns, and subtle
background shifts before adding another card. A card that exists must have a clear purpose.

## 7. Avoid excessive border radius

Not everything gets 24/32/9999px. Buttons 6–12px; inputs 6–10px; larger panels 10–18px; media
frames can be larger when visually appropriate. Do not make every container a soft floating
bubble.

## 8. Pills should be rare

Not for navigation, metadata, statuses, tags, actions, filters, headings, categories, and
icons all at once. Pills only where the semantics fit: status, category, selected filter,
compact toggle. A text link stays a text link.

## 9. Do not overuse icons

No icons merely to look "designed". Avoid icon + title + paragraph repeated six times. Icons
must improve recognition, clarify actions, or reduce cognitive load. No decorative icons where
typography communicates better. One consistent icon family.

## 10. No random bento grid

Asymmetric panels only when content hierarchy supports it. A bento must communicate
importance, grouping, capability, or relationship — not "one large card, two small cards,
another wide card, random gradients."

## 11. Color must come from brand logic

Do not default to dark navy + electric purple + blue glow + white text. Choose from brand
personality, industry, audience, product behavior, emotional tone. Restrained palette: 1
dominant neutral, 1 secondary neutral, 1 brand accent, 1 semantic system
(success/warning/danger/info). Semantic colors are not decoration.

## 12. Avoid gradient abuse

Never automatic. Not gradient text + glowing border + gradient button + gradient background +
blob + icon + card in one interface. A gradient must serve composition, depth, brand, or
focus — not signal "AI".

## 13. Glassmorphism is not a default

Glass only when content sits over imagery/video, transparency preserves continuity, or
hierarchy benefits from depth. No backdrop-blur-xl everywhere. Subtle and limited.

## 14. Use realistic information density

Real products contain metadata, timestamps, secondary actions, statuses, navigation,
constraints, errors, empty states, long text, variable content. Design for actual data, not
perfect demo content. Mentally test with: short names, very long names, empty, loading, error,
1 item, 100 items, long transcripts, multiple actions, small screens.

## 15. Do not center everything

Headline + copy + buttons + screenshots + section headings + features + testimonials all
centered is the AI default. Use left alignment, asymmetric layouts, editorial layouts,
anchored elements, split compositions where appropriate. Centered layouts must be intentional.

## 16. Avoid perfect symmetry everywhere

Human-designed interfaces contain controlled imbalance. Not "3 equal cards" three times per
page. Vary content widths and emphasis where content warrants it.

## 17. Real product logic before visual style

Before designing any screen answer: Who is using it? What do they want to accomplish? What is
the primary action? What information matters first? What states can the product be in? What
happens after the primary action? What can fail? What data is real? What stays hidden until
needed? Design from these answers, not from decoration.

## 18. Every screen needs a clear primary action

Not all buttons equal. Define primary / secondary / tertiary / danger. One primary action
dominates a local context unless there's a strong reason.

## 19. Buttons must not all look the same

Primary: solid. Secondary: subtle fill or border. Tertiary: text. Destructive: reserved danger
styling. Icon-only: only where meaning is obvious.

## 20. No fake product screenshots

Do not invent dashboards full of fake metrics to fill space. Use actual product data; if
unknown, neutral realistic placeholders during development — never fabricated claims as final
content.

## 21. Avoid generic empty states

Not "No data yet!" / "Let's get started!" / "Nothing here… yet." Explain what the area
contains, why it's empty, and what to do next. Example: "No meetings have been captured yet.
Start a meeting or paste a meeting link to create your first recording."

## 22. Loading states should match the product

Not generic spinners everywhere. Prefer skeletons, progressive states, inline status,
contextual loading. "Preparing transcript" instead of "Loading…".

## 23. Error states must be useful

Not only "Something went wrong." Say what failed, whether data is safe, what the user can do,
and offer retry. Example: "We couldn't generate the transcript. The recording is safe. Try
transcription again."

## 24. Not too many shadows

Shadows communicate elevation, overlay, floating interaction, separation. Flat layouts can use
borders, contrast, or spacing instead.

## 25. Avoid the default Tailwind look

Not bg-zinc-950 / border-zinc-800 / rounded-2xl / shadow-xl / text-zinc-400 / bg-purple-500
reflexes. Tailwind is an implementation tool, not a design system. Use the product tokens
(this repo: DESIGN.md + tailwind.config.ts).

## 26. Real spacing system

No random 12/17/23/37px. Use the scale (4 8 12 16 24 32 48 64 96); break it only when
visually justified.

## 27. Consistent component geometry

Buttons, inputs, dropdowns, tabs, cards, modals: consistent heights, padding, radius, border
tone, focus state, typography, icon sizing. One product.

## 28. Do not overanimate

Not everything fades in; no cards floating on hover; no text sliding from random directions;
no spring excess; no scroll-trigger on every section; no parallax everywhere. Motion explains
state change, hierarchy, continuity, spatial relationship.

## 29. Hover effects should be subtle

Not translateY(-8px) + scale(1.03) + glow on every card. A professional hover may change
background tone, border tone, text color, cursor, or a tiny translation.

## 30. Scroll effects need purpose

Reveal progression, preserve continuity, demonstrate the product, communicate hierarchy (e.g.
a hero video shrinking into a framed product demo is meaningful). Random flying elements are
not.

## 31. Use real imagery intentionally

No stock people laughing at laptops, teams pointing at screens, abstract AI brains, robots,
holographic networks. Prefer product UI, actual workflow, diagrams, original illustration,
meaningful editorial imagery, product-specific motion.

## 32. No random decorative orbs

No default circles, glowing balls, floating spheres, blurred blobs, mesh gradients, stars,
sparkle icons, grids, or dotted backgrounds without a clear brand or composition reason.

## 33. Don't overuse badges above headlines

The "[ AI POWERED ] + big headline + paragraph + button" pattern is not mandatory. If an
eyebrow is useful, plain typography usually beats a badge.

## 34. Navigation should feel product-specific

Not automatically "Logo | Product | Features | Pricing | About | Login | Get Started".
Determine what users need. App navigation prioritizes tasks (Home, Meetings, Search, Actions,
People) over generic feature categories.

## 35. Don't copy competitor information architecture

References may inspire spacing, visual language, interaction quality, typography, motion.
Do not copy exact page composition, exact navigation, exact cards, exact hierarchy, exact
copy, exact interactions. The final product must have its own identity.

## 36. Design system first, screen second

Establish typography, colors, spacing, radius, borders, shadows, buttons, inputs, navigation,
tables, cards, modals, statuses, icons — then reuse. Don't redesign the visual language per
screen.

## 37. Microcopy should sound human

"We couldn't save this change." over "Oops! Something went wrong."
"Meeting scheduled." over "You're all set!"
"Record your first meeting." over "Let's get started!" Be concise.

## 38. Not everything friendly and cute

No emoji excess, playful copy everywhere, cartoon illustration, or unnecessary exclamation
marks unless the brand explicitly calls for it. Professional software can feel warm without
feeling childish.

## 39. Reduce exclamation marks

"You're all set!" / "Great!" / "Success!" / "Let's go!" — use sparingly.

## 40. Avoid copy repetition

Not "AI-powered", "smart", "seamless", "powerful", "effortless" on repeat. Explain actual
functionality.

## 41. Responsive design must be intentional

Don't just stack desktop columns. On mobile: reconsider hierarchy, change navigation pattern,
reduce secondary information, preserve primary actions, resize type, reconsider tables, reduce
decorative effects, keep touch targets accessible. Mobile should feel designed, not collapsed.

## 42. Design for accessibility

Readable contrast, visible focus states, keyboard navigation, semantic HTML, proper labels,
sufficient touch targets, usable font sizes. Never sacrifice usability for polish.

## 43. Product UX > Dribbble aesthetics

Optimize for speed, comprehension, confidence, discoverability, task completion — not
screenshots. A beautiful interface that makes the task harder is a failed interface.

## 44. Do not fill space for the sake of it

Empty space can be intentional. No random illustration, meaningless statistic, decorative
card, or fake quote to plug a gap. Reconsider composition instead.

## 45. Content drives component size

Don't force equal-height cards when information differs. Don't truncate meaningful content to
preserve a perfect grid. Allow natural content rhythm.

## 46. Avoid the "AI designer" headline style

Not "Your meetings. Reimagined." / "Less busywork. More impact." / "Capture. Understand.
Act." — short dramatic fragments are heavily overused. Prefer original, specific language.

## 47. Avoid overdesigned dashboards

No five KPI cards + donut + bar chart + activity feed + progress chart unless those metrics
truly matter. A dashboard answers: "What does the user need to do or know right now?"

## 48. Tables are allowed

Tables are often right for dense records, comparison, inventory, meetings, transactions,
users, logs. Use the right information structure, not cards-because-modern.

## 49. Sidebars are for navigation

No huge logo areas, gradients, oversized account cards, decorative badges, nested cards. Keep
navigation efficient.

## 50. Ask before adding a new component type

Before adding a card, badge, accordion, carousel, tabs, tooltip, drawer, or modal ask: "Does
this interaction actually require this component?" Use the simplest correct interaction.

# Required design process

Before generating UI:
1. Understand the product.
2. Identify the primary user.
3. Identify the user's primary task.
4. Define screen hierarchy.
5. Define visual direction.
6. Define design tokens.
7. Identify what makes this product visually unique.
8. Identify the AI-design clichés relevant to this project.
9. Explicitly avoid those clichés.
10. Then design.

# Required self-review (run before presenting any screen)

Copy: Does this sound like a human wrote it? Unnecessary em dashes? Specific to the product?
Invented claims? Generic AI marketing language?

Layout: Everything unnecessarily in cards? Everything centered? Too symmetrical? Does the
hierarchy make sense?

Visuals: Too many gradients? Glows? Rounded rectangles? Pills? Borders? Icons?

UX: Primary action obvious? Loading, empty, error states considered? Works with real data?
Interaction logical?

Brand: Could this design belong to any SaaS company? If yes, redesign. A strong design should
feel identifiable even without the logo.

# Final rule

Do not ask "How can I make this look more modern?" Ask "How would an experienced product
designer make this clearer, more useful, more specific to this product, and visually
coherent?"

Function first. Hierarchy second. Identity third. Decoration last.
